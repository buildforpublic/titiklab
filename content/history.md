# History & Cultural Significance

> **Oral-history source:** Interview by Ayunee with Mahammod Bongsu, founder of Sulimbag Jawtee. The account below reflects the interviewee’s knowledge and lived cultural experience. Details marked for confirmation remain under community review.

## The Bajau community represented here

In this account, Tagunggu' is associated with Bajau cultural practitioners living in Semporna, Sabah. The tradition is rooted in families that practised cultural rituals such as *mag’ombok*. Knowledge was passed from one generation to the next through family and community practice, rather than only through formal teaching.

Historically, not every Bajau community practised the same rituals or traditions. As Tagunggu' developed as a musical practice, it came to be played more widely across Bajau communities.

## What is Tagunggu'?

Tagunggu' is a Bajau traditional musical ensemble centred on the kulintangan, agung gongs and tambul drum.

Tagunggu' is performed for rituals, festivities, weddings, welcoming guests and dignitaries, important commemorations, and the Regatta Lepa festival. In earlier times, gong sounds could call a community together or signal an emergency such as a death. The tradition was also played after a Qur’an completion ceremony during *maglami-lami*.

## The role of the titik

A *titik* is a rhythmic form within the Tagunggu' repertoire. Different titik accompany different styles of *igal* dance and cultural settings. The five core titik documented here are **Tabawan, Tarirai, Limbayan, Lellang, and Luvak-luvak**. **Senai-senai is included as an additional rhythm**, rather than as one of the five core entries.

Mahammod Bongsu estimated that more than 30 rhythmic forms may once have existed, including names such as *Sunat* and *Jawtee*, but the full repertoire can no longer be traced. This estimate requires further documentation.

## Why preservation matters

Tagunggu' carries the legacy of Bajau communities whose cultural practices included *mag’ombok*, *magriawata*, and *magsibandai*. For tradition-bearers, continuing this legacy through future generations is essential.

The interview identified several challenges:

- fewer young people are interested in learning and carrying the tradition forward;
- some ritual observances require substantial cost and preparation;
- fewer active practitioners are bringing the tradition into public view; and
- musical practice can become separated from the customary knowledge held by communities that observe particular annual ritual dates.

Digitisation can document the music, terminology, stories and community testimony before they are lost to modernisation. Digital material should support—not replace—the practitioners and customary holders who sustain the living tradition.
